> **The short answer**
> An AI marketing system has five parts: a knowledge base of what's true about your business, standing instructions for how AI should work, skills that each do one job, workflows that chain skills with a person approving at each checkpoint, and a review loop. Write it down once. Use it everywhere.

> **Three takeaways**
> - **Prompts start from zero every time.** A system saves what AI needs to know so nobody pastes it in again.
> - **Build in order.** Knowledge base, standing instructions, skills, workflows, then the review loop.
> - **Your first skill is the weekly task someone complains about.**

<!-- toc -->

Look closely at how teams at Level 4 on the AI fluency ladder work, and you'll see the same thing happen over and over. Someone opens an AI tool. They paste in the persona again. They explain the brand voice again. They rewrite the prompt that worked last month, from memory, a little worse.

Every task starts from zero. Rinse and repeat.

A system fixes that.

## What is an AI marketing system?

**A set of saved, connected tools that make AI work the same way every time, with a person in charge.** You write down what AI needs to know about your business once. You turn your best prompts into small tools that do one job well. You connect those tools into workflows, with a person signing off at the points that matter. Then you measure it and improve it every quarter, the same way you manage everything else.

The answers you wrote in your strategy, buyer and competitor work are the raw material. Your goals, customers, competitors, voice and numbers are already on paper. This is how you put them to work.

## Who is this for?

**Teams at Level 4.** That means AI is already a written step in your workflows, and a person approves what it produces. If you're at Level 2 or 3, start with the AI prompts for your focus areas and come back when AI is part of how your team works every week.

AI agents come after the system works, not before.

## What are the five parts?

**Knowledge base, standing instructions, skills, workflows and a review loop.** Each makes the others work.

1. **Knowledge base.** What's true about your business.
2. **Standing instructions.** How AI should work for your team.
3. **Skills.** One job each, saved and reused.
4. **Workflows.** Skills chained in order, with a named person approving at each checkpoint.
5. **Review loop.** Log every skill, fix misses at the source and review everything each quarter.

You build them in that order.

## What goes in a knowledge base?

**A small set of short documents that tell AI who you are.** One to three pages each. AI works better with clear, current facts than with everything you've ever written. The documents in the book are:

- **Business basics:** what you sell, to whom, where and at what price, plus your one-year goal and KPIs.
- **Customers:** personas, the questions buyers ask at each stage and the words they use.
- **Competitors:** who they are, what they promise and where they're weak.
- **Voice:** how you sound, words you use and avoid, and two or three samples of your best writing.
- **Proof:** results, reviews, case studies and facts you can stand behind, each with its source.
- **Offers and channels:** your services, current offers and the channels you use.
- **Rules:** your AI usage policy, covering what data AI may see and what people must approve.

Leave customer names, contact details, payment information and personnel records out. Date every document and give it an owner. A knowledge base that's six months out of date will confidently tell customers about last year's prices.

## What are standing instructions?

**The rules your AI tool follows in every conversation.** Your knowledge base says what's true. Standing instructions say how to work. Keep them under a page. They cover five things:

1. **Who we are.** One paragraph: the business, the customer, the goal.
2. **How we sound.** Three or four rules, like "short sentences" and "no hype."
3. **What's true.** Use only facts from the knowledge base or that I give you. If you're not sure, say so and ask. Never invent statistics, quotes or results.
4. **What stays private.** Which data AI may not see or repeat.
5. **What comes back to a person.** Mark anything that will be published, sent to a customer or spent as money as "DRAFT FOR APPROVAL."

Test your instructions on something you've written yourself. Where the draft misses, add a rule.

## What is an AI skill?

**A saved, reusable set of instructions for one task.** Write a review reply. Draft a search ad. Summarize last month's numbers. Instead of rewriting the prompt each time, you call the skill, give it today's details and get a consistent first draft.

The idea is simple and it scales. Some teams break a single job into 20 or more small skills. No single skill is impressive. Together they're a system.

Before you build one, write it on a card: a short name, when to use it and what it does. If you can't fill in the card, the skill isn't ready. Most AI tools now let you save skills under different names and plans. A shared document of tested prompts works too. Start there if your tool doesn't support skills yet.

**Your first skill should be the weekly task someone on your team complains about.** Build the system around work you already do.

## Where do people fit in?

**At the checkpoints, and they stay there.** A workflow chains skills in order, and a named person approves at each step that matters. That's the same rule from the first chapter of the book: you think, AI drafts, you decide.

## Your next three actions

1. Write one knowledge base document this week, starting with your customers and the questions they ask.
2. Write your standing instructions in under a page, and test them on something you've written yourself.
3. Pick the weekly task someone complains about, and turn it into your first skill.
