> **The short answer**
> Count customers in your own CRM by original source, not the sales each platform claims. Then add one question to every form and first call: "How did you find out about us?" That's how you see what the software can't.

> **Three takeaways**
> - **Platforms grade their own homework.** Add up what each one claims and you'll often get more conversions than you actually made.
> - **Your CRM is the source of truth.** Save where each lead first came from, and don't let later visits overwrite it.
> - **If a number won't change a decision, don't report it.** Pick one or two measures per discipline you're working on.

Most small businesses don't have a data problem. They have plenty of data. It's spread across Google Analytics, Search Console, ad platforms, email tools, social accounts, the CRM and the sales team's memory.

What's missing is a clear set of goals, a few measures that match them and a regular habit of looking at them together and deciding what to do. Let's build that.

## Why do my ad platforms add up to more sales than I made?

**Because every platform grades its own homework.** Google Ads, Meta and your email tool each count a sale when their own ad or message touched the buyer. If one customer clicked a Google ad, saw a Facebook ad and opened your email, all three may report the same sale.

Meta can also count people who saw an ad but never clicked it, so check which window your reports use.

That doesn't make platform numbers useless. They're good for comparing ads inside one platform. They're poor for deciding how to split your budget between platforms. For that, use your own records.

## What should I measure in my CRM?

**Where each lead first came from.** When a new contact fills in a form for the first time, save the tagged-link (UTM) source, medium and campaign, the landing page and the answer to "How did you find out about us?" Store these in fields that later visits don't overwrite.

Most CRMs can do this with hidden form fields or a built-in "original source" field. Some let users edit the original source, so check yours.

At a minimum, make sure every lead records where it came from, and every sale records which lead it came from. Google Analytics can show where a visitor first arrived. It can't follow that person across devices or tie them to a sale your team closes months later. Your CRM can.

## What can't my analytics see?

**More than you'd think.** Buyers research in AI answers, private messages, podcasts and conversations with friends. None of that shows up in your analytics.

Here's an example. A buyer asks ChatGPT for a recommendation, then types your name into Google. In your reports, that looks like "direct" or "branded search" traffic. The real source never appears.

What you can see: website visits, ad clicks, email clicks, form fills. What you can't: AI answers, word of mouth, private messages, review sites.

You close that gap with one question.

## How do I close the gap?

**Ask every lead.** Add "How did you find out about us?" to every form and every first call. Use an open text box, not a drop-down. Record the answers in your CRM.

Self-reported answers aren't perfect. They do reveal the channels software misses.

If you also use offline channels, connect them to your data with unique web addresses, QR codes and call tracking. Radio didn't kill print, and TV didn't kill radio. Measure them alongside everything else.

## What are the four pillars of measurement?

**They're from the first edition, and they still hold up.**

1. **Set clear objectives and the right metrics.** Start with the goals from your strategy. For each goal, choose the few measures that show progress. If a number won't change a decision, don't report it.
2. **Collect and connect your data.** Connect your tools so you can follow a buyer from first visit to sale.
3. **Analyze it, with AI's help.** Export a report, ask AI what changed and why, check its answer against the data and put the result in a simple chart.
4. **Build a data-driven culture.** Tools don't change decisions. People do. Share results openly with marketing, sales and leadership. Celebrate learning from tests that failed, not just the ones that worked.

## Can I trust AI to analyze my numbers?

**Only if you check its work.** AI tools will confidently analyze numbers they misread, and sometimes invent numbers that aren't in your data. Give AI the data, ask it to show its work and treat its conclusions as a draft for a person to verify. That's ground rule two in the book: verify facts.

## Which KPIs should I track?

**One or two for each discipline you're actively working on.** Don't track them all. A few examples from the book:

- **SEO and AI search:** impressions, clicks and position for priority searches, plus AI mentions and citations.
- **Paid media:** cost per lead, cost per sale and return on ad spend, calculated from your own books.
- **Website:** conversion rate by page and source, and form completion rate.
- **Local:** Google Business Profile calls, directions and website clicks, plus review count, rating and how recent the reviews are.
- **Analytics itself:** the share of leads with a known source, and the gap between the conversions your platforms claim and the customers in your CRM.

And across all of them, the business numbers that matter most: cost per acquisition, average order or project value, customer lifetime value and return on marketing investment.

## Your next three actions

1. Add "How did you find out about us?" to every form and to your first-call notes this week.
2. Check that your CRM saves the original source of every new lead and doesn't overwrite it.
3. Compare the conversions your platforms claim last month with the new customers in your CRM. The gap is your first lesson.
